Reported speech, also called indirect speech, is when we tell someone what another person said without using their exact words. Instead of repeating their words word-for-word, we report the meaning of what they said. This requires changing several elements of the original sentence, including the verb tense, pronouns, and sometimes time expressions.

## When We Use Reported Speech

We use reported speech when we want to communicate someone's message after they have spoken, rather than quoting them directly. Common situations include telling others about a conversation you had, reporting what someone told you, or sharing information from a meeting or phone call. For example, instead of saying "He said, 'I am tired,'" we might say "He said he was tired." Reported speech is more common in everyday conversation than direct speech, especially when we are not trying to emphasize the speaker's exact words.

## The Basic Structure

Reported speech follows this pattern: subject + reporting verb + that + reported clause. The most common reporting verb is say or tell. When you use tell, you must include an object (the person being told): She told me that she was leaving. When you use say, you do not need an object, but you can include one with to: She said that she was leaving, or She said to me that she was leaving. The word that is often optional and can be left out: She said she was leaving.

## Changes in Verb Tense

When the reporting verb is in the past tense, the verb in the reported clause usually moves back one tense. Present simple becomes past simple: He said, "I like coffee." becomes He said he liked coffee. Present continuous becomes past continuous: She said, "I am reading." becomes She said she was reading. Present perfect becomes past perfect: They said, "We have finished." becomes They said they had finished. Past simple becomes past perfect: He said, "I went home." becomes He said he had gone home. However, if the reported speech is about something that is still true, you can keep the original tense: She said she is a doctor. This is acceptable because being a doctor is a current fact.

## Changes in Pronouns and References

Pronouns change depending on who is reporting the speech. If the original speaker said "I am happy," the person reporting must change "I" to "he" or "she" depending on the speaker's gender. Similarly, "you" may change to "I" if the original listener is now the one reporting: He asked, "Will you help me?" becomes He asked if I would help him. The pronouns "he," "she," and "it" usually stay the same unless the context makes a change necessary. Possessive adjectives also change: She said, "My car is broken." becomes She said her car was broken.

## Changes in Time Expressions

Time expressions often change when reporting speech because the time of reporting is different from the time of the original statement. Today becomes that day: He said, "I will see you today." becomes He said he would see me that day. Tomorrow becomes the next day or the following day: She said, "I will call you tomorrow." becomes She said she would call me the next day. Yesterday becomes the day before: He said, "I saw her yesterday." becomes He said he had seen her the day before. Now becomes then: She said, "I am leaving now." becomes She said she was leaving then. This week, this month, and this year can become that week, that month, or that year. However, if the context is still the same, some expressions can stay unchanged: He said, "2025 is an important year." can remain He said 2025 is an important year.

## Reported Questions

When reporting a yes/no question, use if or whether: He asked, "Are you coming?" becomes He asked if I was coming. For questions with question words like what, why, when, or who, keep the question word at the beginning of the reported clause: She asked, "Where are you going?" becomes She asked where I was going. Remember that reported questions use statement word order, not question word order. The structure is: subject + reporting verb + question word + subject + verb. So "Do you like pizza?" becomes "She asked if I liked pizza," not "She asked do I like pizza." The verb still moves back one tense following the same rules as reported statements.

## Reported Negative Sentences

When reporting a negative statement, the negative word not moves with the verb in the reported clause. He said, "I don't like spicy food." becomes He said he didn't like spicy food. If the reported clause contains a negative, the reporting clause can sometimes be negative too: She said, "I won't help you." can become She said she wouldn't help me, or She said she wouldn't help me (both are correct). In some cases, you might move the negative to the reporting verb itself: He said, "I don't know the answer." can become He said he didn't know the answer, or He didn't say he knew the answer (though this changes the meaning slightly).

## Common Learner Mistakes

One frequent error is forgetting to change the verb tense. Learners often write "He said he likes coffee" when they should write "He said he liked coffee." Another mistake is failing to change pronouns correctly. For example, writing "She said I am tired" instead of "She said she was tired." Some learners incorrectly keep the question word order in reported questions, saying "She asked what was I doing" instead of "She asked what I was doing." Time expressions are also problematic. Learners sometimes forget to change today to that day or tomorrow to the next day. Additionally, learners may use the reporting verb in the present tense (She says she is tired) when the original conversation happened in the past. Finally, many learners overuse the word that or forget it entirely when it is actually helpful for clarity. While that is optional, including it often makes the sentence easier to understand, especially in longer or more complex sentences.
