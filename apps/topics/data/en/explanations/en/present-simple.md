The present simple is the most basic tense in English. It expresses facts, habits, routines, and states that are generally true. You use it for actions that happen regularly or permanently, without a specific connection to the moment of speaking.

## When we use the present simple

- For facts and permanent situations: The Earth orbits the sun. My sister lives in London.
- For habits and routines: I drink coffee every morning. He plays tennis on Thursdays.
- For general truths: Water boils at 100 degrees. Birds have wings.
- For scheduled events in the future: The train leaves at 3 p.m. tomorrow. The meeting starts at 9 a.m.
- In stories and sports commentary to create immediacy: Hamlet enters the stage. The striker shoots and scores!
- For states and feelings: I like pizza. She knows French. They seem happy.

## Forming the positive

- With I, you, we, they: Use the base form of the verb. I work. You eat. We understand. They play.
- With he, she, it: Add -s or -es to the base form. He works. She eats. It flies. The baby cries.
- The basic pattern is: Subject + base verb (or base verb + -s/-es for third person).
- Examples: I play tennis. She plays tennis. We watch films. It watches us.

## Forming the negative

- Add do not (don't) or does not (doesn't) before the main verb.
- With I, you, we, they: I do not work here. You don't understand. They don't sleep.
- With he, she, it: He does not work here. She doesn't understand. It doesn't sleep.
- The main verb returns to its base form: He does not works is wrong. He does not work is correct.
- Examples: I don't like cheese. She doesn't speak Spanish. We don't go out on Mondays.

## Forming the question

- Invert the subject and the auxiliary verb do or does.
- With I, you, we, they: Do you understand? Do they play football? Do I need a ticket?
- With he, she, it: Does he work here? Does she know the answer? Does it matter?
- The main verb stays in the base form: Does he works? is wrong. Does he work? is correct.
- Examples: Do you like this music? Does she speak English? Do they live nearby?

## Spelling and morphology rules

- Most verbs simply add -s: work becomes works. Play becomes plays.
- Verbs ending in -ch, -sh, -ss, -x, or -z add -es: watch becomes watches. Push becomes pushes. Pass becomes passes.
- Verbs ending in consonant + -y change the -y to -i and add -es: cry becomes cries. Study becomes studies. Carry becomes carries.
- Verbs ending in vowel + -y just add -s: play becomes plays. Enjoy becomes enjoys.
- The verb be is irregular: I am. You are. He/she/it is. We are. They are.
- The verb have is mostly regular (has for third person) but used frequently: I have. She has.

## Time expressions and markers

- Adverbs of frequency often accompany the present simple: always, usually, sometimes, rarely, never. I always drink tea in the morning. She rarely goes to parties.
- Expressions with "every": every day, every week, every year, every Monday. I see my parents every Sunday.
- Expressions with "once, twice": once a week. Twice a month. He visits twice a year.
- General time markers: in the morning, in the evening, at night, at 9 o'clock. She leaves at 6 a.m.
- Present simple can refer to the future with scheduled events: The plane arrives at noon tomorrow.

## Common learner mistakes

- Forgetting the -s/-es for third person: He go to school is wrong. He goes to school is correct.
- Using the base verb with does: Does he goes? is wrong. Does he go? is correct.
- Mixing auxiliary verbs with tenses: I doesn't like this is wrong. I don't like this is correct.
- Placing the negative in the wrong position: I like not coffee is wrong. I don't like coffee is correct.
- Confusing the present simple with the present continuous: I work in an office describes a general fact. I am working in an office describes an action happening right now.
- Forgetting that frequency adverbs come between the subject and the verb or after the auxiliary: She usually eats lunch at noon is correct. She eats usually lunch is wrong.
- Using the present simple for one-time actions in progress: I am reading a book right now is correct. I read a book right now is wrong, because you need the present continuous for actions happening at this moment.
