The present perfect continuous describes an action that started in the past, continues to the present moment, and may continue into the future. It emphasizes the duration or ongoing nature of the activity and connects past actions to present results or situations.

## Form and Structure

- The present perfect continuous uses have or has plus been plus the present participle (verb + ing).
- Subject + have/has + been + verb-ing
- I have been waiting. She has been working. They have been studying.
- Use has with third person singular subjects (he, she, it, the student). Use have with all other subjects.

## Positive Statements

- I have been cooking since three o'clock.
- You have been running for two hours.
- He has been learning English for five years.
- We have been waiting in the queue all morning.
- They have been traveling across Europe for three weeks.

## Negative Statements and Questions

- Negatives add not between have/has and been: I have not been sleeping well. She has not been paying attention.
- Short forms use haven't and hasn't: They haven't been talking to each other. It hasn't been working properly.
- For questions, move have or has before the subject: Have you been feeling tired? Has he been practicing? How long have they been living here?
- Question forms with question words: What have you been doing? Where has she been going? Why have they been complaining?

## Time Markers and Duration Expressions

- For is the most common time marker: I have been working here for three months. We have been friends for years.
- Since marks the starting point: She has been teaching since 2010. They have been engaged since last summer.
- All morning, all day, all week, all year describe complete periods: I have been studying all afternoon.
- How long questions: How long have you been living in this apartment? How long has he been playing guitar?
- Recently, lately, just recently appear with present perfect continuous: I have been feeling stressed lately. She has been getting better recently.

## Differences from Present Perfect Simple

- The continuous form emphasizes the duration and ongoing activity: I have been working for two hours (focus on the continuing activity). The simple form emphasizes completion or a recent result: I have worked for two hours and now I am finished (focus on completion).
- Use continuous when the activity is still happening or the duration matters: How long have you been waiting? Use simple when counting completed actions or final results: How many jobs have you had?
- We have been renovating the house (the work is still ongoing). We have renovated three rooms (the work on those rooms is complete).

## Common Learner Mistakes

- Confusing with present perfect simple: Incorrect: I have worked here for five years. Correct: I have been working here for five years (if still employed there).
- Using the simple present instead: Incorrect: I am working here for five years. Correct: I have been working here for five years.
- Wrong time marker: Incorrect: I have been working since three hours. Correct: I have been working for three hours.
- Forgetting been: Incorrect: I have working since morning. Correct: I have been working since morning.
- Using it with completed, finished actions: Incorrect: I have been painted the room (implying ongoing). Say instead: I have painted the room (the work is done).
- Present participle errors: Incorrect: I have been work. Correct: I have been working. Remember all -ing forms: studying, running, sitting, shopping.

## Spelling and Morphology Notes

- One-syllable verbs ending in a single vowel plus a consonant double the final consonant: sit becomes sitting, run becomes running, stop becomes stopping.
- Verbs ending in e drop the e before adding ing: make becomes making, take becomes taking, write becomes writing.
- Verbs ending in ie change to y before adding ing: lie becomes lying, tie becomes tying, die becomes dying.
- Most other verbs simply add ing: work becomes working, play becomes playing, cook becomes cooking.
- Irregular verbs follow the same ing rule (the irregularity is in the base form, not the participle): go becomes going, have becomes having, see becomes seeing.
