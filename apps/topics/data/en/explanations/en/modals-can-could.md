Can and could are modal verbs used to talk about ability, permission, and possibility in English. These modals are fundamental in everyday conversation and writing, especially when learners need to express what they can do, what was possible in the past, or what they are allowed to do.

## Present ability with can

Use can to express what someone is able to do right now or in general. This is the most common use of can.

- Sarah can speak three languages fluently.
- I can cook pasta, but I cannot make bread.
- Can you drive a car? Yes, I can.
- The children can swim in the pool every summer.

Can expresses a general ability or skill that exists at present. It shows what is possible for someone because they have the knowledge or physical ability.

## Past ability with could

Use could to talk about what someone was able to do in the past. Could is the past tense form of can when discussing ability over a period of time or in general situations.

- When I was younger, I could run five kilometers without stopping.
- My grandmother could play the piano beautifully.
- Could you speak English when you were a child? No, I could not.
- Last year, they could visit us every weekend, but now they are too busy.

Could also expresses ability that continued or was repeated in the past. However, when something was actually completed at a specific moment in the past, use was able to instead. Compare: I could swim when I was five years old, but I was able to swim across the river when I was ten (completed action).

## Permission, possibility, and requests with can and could

Can expresses permission or asks permission in informal situations. Could is more polite or formal when asking for permission or making requests.

- Can I sit here? Yes, you can.
- Could you help me with this exercise? That would be very kind.
- You can borrow my book, but you must return it by Friday.
- Could I ask you a question? Of course.

In formal or polite contexts, especially when making a request, could is preferred. Can sounds more direct and casual. Students often forget this distinction and use can for everything.

## Building positive, negative, and question forms

Can and could follow the same pattern as other modals. They do not change form with different subjects, and they are always followed by the base form of the verb.

Positive: subject + can or could + base verb. I can help you. She could dance well. They can arrive tomorrow.

Negative: subject + cannot or could not + base verb. You cannot leave early. We could not find the key. He cannot speak Russian.

Question: can or could + subject + base verb? Can you cook? Could they understand the lesson? Can I use your phone?

Negative questions follow the same pattern. Cannot you come? Could he not visit? These forms exist but are less common in modern English.

## Spelling and contractions

Can is the same in all forms and never changes. The negative form is either cannot, written as one word, or can not, written as two words, though cannot is more standard in modern usage.

Could is the past form of can. The negative is could not or couldn't. Contractions are common in speech and informal writing: can't, couldn't.

Students sometimes write "could of" or "can of" instead of "could not" or "cannot." This is incorrect. The confusion comes from how the contraction sounds in speech, but the written form must use the modal and the verb.

## Time markers and context clues

Present ability uses can with no time marker needed, or with present time words: now, today, at the moment, in general.

Past ability uses could with past time markers: yesterday, last year, when I was young, in those days, back then.

When you see a past time marker in the sentence, use could instead of can. Many learners forget to change the form when they shift to the past tense.

## Common learner mistakes

Wrong: I can speak English when I was a child. Right: I could speak English when I was a child. The learner kept can even though the time reference is past.

Wrong: Can I to help you? Right: Can I help you? Modals are followed directly by the base verb without to.

Wrong: She cans speak French. Right: She can speak French. Modals never take an s form, even with he, she, or it.

Wrong: I can to do this. Right: I can do this. Do not add to after the modal.

Wrong: Could you to come tomorrow? Right: Could you come tomorrow? Again, the base verb follows immediately after the modal.
