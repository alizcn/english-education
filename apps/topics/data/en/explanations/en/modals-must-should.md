Modals are special verbs that express ideas like obligation, advice, possibility, or permission. Must and should are two important modals that English learners often confuse because they both express some kind of obligation or recommendation, but they have different strengths and uses.

## Must: Obligation and Necessity

Use must when you need to express a strong obligation or necessity. Something is required or very important to do. Must shows that something is not optional.

- You must arrive on time. (This is required.)
- Students must complete their homework before class. (This is a rule.)
- You must see a doctor if you have severe pain. (This is necessary for your health.)
- We must protect the environment. (This is essential.)

Use must also to express a logical conclusion or what you think is certainly true.

- She has not eaten all day. She must be very hungry. (You are sure about this.)
- That noise is so loud. Someone must be using power tools. (You believe this is true.)

## Must: Negative Forms and Questions

To make must negative, use must not (spelled as one word or two). Mustn't is common in speech but less formal in writing.

- You must not enter this room. (It is forbidden.)
- Students must not use their phones during the exam. (This is prohibited.)

To ask a question with must, put must before the subject.

- Must we arrive exactly on time? (Is this required?)
- Must students submit their work by Friday? (Is this a requirement?)

Important: Must not means something is forbidden, not that it is optional. If something is optional or not necessary, use do not have to instead.

- You do not have to attend the meeting. (Attendance is optional.)
- Students do not have to buy the expensive textbook. (It is not required.)

## Should: Advice and Recommendation

Use should when you want to give advice, make a recommendation, or express what is the right or correct thing to do. Should is weaker than must. It suggests what is a good idea, but the person can choose not to do it.

- You should eat more vegetables. (This is good advice.)
- Students should study before taking a test. (This is recommended.)
- You should apologize for your mistake. (This is the right thing to do.)
- Parents should talk to their children about school. (This is important but not mandatory.)

Use should not or shouldn't for negative advice.

- You shouldn't stay up too late before an important day. (This is not a good idea.)
- You shouldn't spend all your money on one thing. (This is not wise.)

To ask a question with should, put should before the subject.

- Should I call him first? (Is this a good idea?)
- Should we invite everyone to the party? (What is recommended?)

## The Difference Between Must and Should

Must is much stronger than should. Must means you have no choice. Should means it is a good idea or recommended, but you can decide.

- You must pay your taxes. (The law requires this. You have no choice.)
- You should pay your taxes early. (It is recommended, but this is softer.)
- You must not drink and drive. (This is forbidden and illegal.)
- You shouldn't drink too much. (This is advice for your health.)

In questions, must suggests obligation or rule. Should is asking for an opinion about whether something is wise.

- Must you leave? (Is this a requirement?)
- Should you leave? (Is this a good idea?)

## Common Mistakes Learners Make

Many learners confuse must and should. Here are common errors:

- Wrong: You should wear a seatbelt in a car. (In most places this is the law, so must is correct.)
- Correct: You must wear a seatbelt in a car.
- Wrong: You must eat fruit if you want good health. (This is advice, not a requirement.)
- Correct: You should eat fruit if you want good health.

Another common mistake is forgetting the negative difference.

- Wrong: You must not go to class if you are sick. (This sounds like it is forbidden.)
- Better: You do not have to go to class if you are sick. (This means it is optional.)

Learners also sometimes add to after must or should, or use the wrong verb form.

- Wrong: You should to call your mother. (Extra to is incorrect.)
- Correct: You should call your mother.
- Wrong: You must going to work every day. (Incorrect -ing form.)
- Correct: You must go to work every day.

Some learners try to use must for past obligation, but must does not work in the past. Use had to instead.

- Wrong: When I was a child, I must do my homework every day.
- Correct: When I was a child, I had to do my homework every day.

## Time Expressions and Past Forms

Must and should usually refer to the present and future. They express what is necessary or recommended now or soon.

- You must finish your work by tomorrow. (Future obligation.)
- You should call your parents today. (Present recommendation.)

When you talk about the past, must and should do not work alone. Use had to for past obligation.

- I had to leave early because I was sick. (Past obligation that happened.)

For past recommendation or criticism, use should have with the past participle of the verb.

- You should have called earlier. (You did not call, but it would have been better if you had.)
- He should have studied harder for the test. (This is what would have been wise to do.)
- They should have waited for us. (It would have been good if they waited, but they did not.)
