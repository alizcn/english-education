Turkish uses a system of case suffixes attached to nouns to show their grammatical function in a sentence. These suffixes tell you whether a noun is the subject, object, possessor, recipient, location, or source. Turkish has six main cases. Understanding cases is essential because Turkish word order is more flexible than English, and cases do much of the work that prepositions do in English.

## What Are the Six Cases?

Turkish has six cases: nominative (base form, no suffix), accusative (direct object), genitive (possession), dative (recipient or destination), locative (location), and ablative (source or origin). Each case has specific suffixes that attach to the noun according to vowel harmony rules. Cases remain the same whether a sentence is positive, negative, or a question—the grammatical function of the noun does not change.

- Nominative: Ev. (A house.)
- Accusative: Evi seviyorum. (I love the house.)
- Genitive: Evin kapısı. (The house's door.)
- Dative: Eve gittim. (I went to the house.)
- Locative: Evde oturuyorum. (I am at the house.)
- Ablative: Evden çıktım. (I came from the house.)

## Accusative Case: Marking the Direct Object

The accusative case marks the direct object of a verb—what or whom the verb acts upon. Add suffixes -ı, -i, -u, or -ü after a consonant, or -yı, -yi, -yu, -yü after a vowel, following vowel harmony.

- Kitabı okudum. (I read the book.)
- Arabayı yıkadım. (I washed the car.)
- Kızı gördüm. (I saw the girl.)

Important: The accusative is not used if the object is indefinite and the action is habitual or general. Compare Kitap okuyorum (I read books—general habit) with Kitabı okudum (I read the specific book). The accusative appears the same in negative and question forms: Kitabı okumadım mı? (Didn't I read the book?)

## Genitive Case: Showing Possession

The genitive case shows that one noun belongs to or is associated with another. Use suffixes -in, -ın, -un, or -ün after a consonant, or -nin, -nın, -nun, -nün after a vowel.

- Ayşenin kitabı. (Ayşe's book.)
- Köpeğin adı. (The dog's name.)
- Masanın üstü. (The top of the table.)

The genitive often appears before another noun in a possessive construction. The possessed noun usually takes a possessive suffix (-i, -ı, -u, -ü, -si, -sı, -su, -sü) as well. The structure works the same in negative and question sentences: Ayşenin kitabı değil mi? (Is it not Ayşe's book?)

## The Three Spatial Cases: Dative, Locative, and Ablative

Turkish has three cases for spatial relationships. The dative (-a/-e/-ya/-ye) marks destination or recipient. The locative (-de/-da/-te/-ta) shows location or position. The ablative (-den/-dan/-ten/-tan) indicates movement from a place or origin.

- Dative: Ona mektup gönderdim. (I sent a letter to him/her.) Türkiyeye gidiyorum. (I am going to Turkey.)
- Locative: Masada kitap var. (There is a book on the table.) Türkiyede yaşıyorum. (I live in Turkey.)
- Ablative: Evden ayrıldı. (He/she left from the house.) Istanbuldan buraya geldim. (I came here from Istanbul.)

Many verbs require the dative for their complement (vermek, söylemek, yardım etmek). These cases function identically in negative sentences and questions: Istanbuldan mı geldin? (Did you come from Istanbul?)

## Vowel Harmony and Consonant Assimilation

Turkish case suffixes follow strict vowel harmony rules. Back vowels (a, ı, o, u) pair with back vowel suffixes; front vowels (e, i, ö, ü) pair with front vowel suffixes. After vowels, certain suffixes gain a consonant (y, n, or d) to separate adjacent vowels.

- After consonants: ev (house) becomes eve, evde, evden
- After back vowels: kız (girl) becomes kıza, kızda, kızdan
- After front vowels: köpek (dog) becomes köpeğe, köpekte, köpekten

When a word ends in k, p, ç, or t, these consonants become soft (ğ, b, c, d) before suffixes with front vowels. For example, köpek (dog) becomes köpeğe (dative).

## Common Learner Mistakes

Many learners forget to apply vowel harmony consistently or confuse which suffix belongs to which case. A very common mistake is omitting the accusative suffix with definite objects. Another frequent error is using the wrong case after verbs that require a specific case.

- Wrong: Kütüphaneye kitap verdim. Correct: Kütüphaneye kitabı verdim. (I gave the book to the library.)
- Wrong: Istanbul gittim. Correct: Istanbula gittim. (I went to Istanbul.)
- Wrong: Onun sözü dinle. Correct: Onun sözünü dinle. (Listen to his/her words.)

Also, learners sometimes confuse the locative suffix -de with the postposition de meaning "also." The locative suffix is stressed and attached directly; de is typically unstressed and can appear with any word. Remember that the case suffixes work the same in positive statements, negative statements, and questions.
