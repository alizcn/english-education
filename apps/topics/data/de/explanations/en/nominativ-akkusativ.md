The Nominative and Accusative are two of the four German cases. The Nominative marks the subject of a sentence, while the Accusative marks the direct object. These are the most frequently used cases, and controlling them well will make an enormous difference in your German.

## The Nominativ: What and When

The Nominativ is the case of the subject—the person or thing performing the action. To identify the subject, ask "Wer?" (Who?) or "Was?" (What?). The subject always comes with the nominative article or pronoun.

- Der Mann arbeitet. (The man works—der Mann is the subject.)
- Ich bin ein Lehrer. (I am a teacher—ich is the subject.)
- Das Kind spielt im Park. (The child plays in the park—das Kind is the subject.)
- Wer kommt heute? Mein Freund kommt heute. (Who comes today? My friend comes today.)

## The Akkusativ: What and When

The Akkusativ is the case of the direct object—the person or thing that receives the action. To identify the direct object, ask "Wen?" (Whom?) or "Was?" (What?). Not all verbs take a direct object; only transitive verbs do. Many very common verbs require the accusative.

- Ich sehe den Mann. (I see the man—den Mann receives the action of seeing.)
- Sie kauft einen Kaffee. (She buys a coffee—einen Kaffee receives the action of buying.)
- Wir lieben unsere Familie. (We love our family—unsere Familie receives the action of loving.)
- Wen besuchst du? Ich besuche meinen Großvater. (Whom do you visit? I visit my grandfather.)

Common verbs that take the accusative include: sehen, haben, kaufen, lieben, essen, trinken, lesen, schreiben, finden, suchen, verstehen, kennen, and many others.

## How Articles and Adjectives Change

Only masculine singular articles and adjectives change form between nominative and accusative. Feminine and neuter articles remain the same. Nouns themselves do not change.

The definite article:
- Nominativ: der Mann, die Frau, das Kind
- Akkusativ: den Mann, die Frau, das Kind

The indefinite article:
- Nominativ: ein Mann, eine Frau, ein Kind
- Akkusativ: einen Mann, eine Frau, ein Kind

Adjectives with definite articles:
- Nominativ: Der alte Mann sitzt hier. (The old man sits here.)
- Akkusativ: Ich sehe den alten Mann. (I see the old man.)
- Nominativ: Das kleine Kind schläft. (The small child sleeps.)
- Akkusativ: Ich finde das kleine Kind süß. (I find the small child sweet.)

Adjectives with indefinite articles:
- Nominativ: Ein guter Freund ist wichtig. (A good friend is important.)
- Akkusativ: Ich habe einen guten Freund. (I have a good friend.)

Personal pronouns change in both nominative and accusative:
- Nominativ: ich, du, er, sie, es, wir, ihr, sie, Sie
- Akkusativ: mich, dich, ihn, sie, es, uns, euch, sie, Sie

Notice that feminine, neuter, and plural pronouns do not change: sie, es, sie, Sie.

## Recognizing Cases in Sentences

The accusative and nominative are distinguished by the article or the pronoun, not by word order. Even when the direct object comes before the subject in a question, the article tells you which is which.

- Wen siehst du? (Whom do you see?) [wen shows this is accusative, even though it is first]
- Der Lehrer sieht den Schüler. (The teacher sees the student.) [der = subject, den = object]
- Den Schüler sieht der Lehrer. (The student—the teacher sees him.) [accusative object comes first, but den shows it is the object]

Word order alone does not tell you the case; only the article or pronoun does. This is why articles are so important in German.

## Common Mistakes Learners Make

English speakers especially struggle with nominative and accusative because English uses word order to show subject and object, not articles.

- Writing "Ich sehe der Mann" instead of "Ich sehe den Mann." The definite article must be accusative after the verb sehen.

- Using "Ich bin einen Schüler" instead of "Ich bin ein Schüler." The verb sein requires nominative for both the subject and the complement, never accusative.

- Overgeneralizing the accusative change. Learners sometimes write "Ich sehe die Kind" instead of "Ich sehe das Kind," forgetting that only the masculine singular definite article changes from der to den.

- Neglecting weak masculine nouns. Some masculine nouns add -en or -n in accusative, dative, and genitive (for example, der Student becomes den Studenten). This is a more advanced topic but worth noting.

- Forgetting pronoun changes. Writing "Ich sehe er" instead of "Ich sehe ihn" is a frequent beginner mistake.

- Confusing sein with transitive verbs. The complement of sein is always nominative: "Er ist der Lehrer" (He is the teacher), not "Er ist den Lehrer."
