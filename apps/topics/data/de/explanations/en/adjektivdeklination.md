German adjectives change their endings depending on the gender, number, and case of the noun they describe, as well as the article or determiner that precedes them. This system is called Adjektivdeklination, and while it appears complex, it follows predictable patterns once you understand the three main declination types: with a definite article, with an indefinite article, and without an article.

## When adjectives need to change

Adjectives must decline whenever they come before a noun in the attributive position. Das große Haus is different from Der Mann ist groß because in the first example the adjective "große" comes before the noun and must show agreement, while in the second example "groß" is a predicate adjective and does not change. You decline an adjective even if it feels awkward or unnecessary in English—German requires this agreement to indicate case, gender, and number clearly.

## The three declination systems

German has three main contexts for adjective endings. With the definite article (der, die, das, den, etc.), adjectives generally follow one set of rules. With the indefinite article (ein, eine, ein, einen, etc.), they follow different rules. With no article at all—called bare or unpreceded adjectives—they take yet another pattern. Each system serves to clarify the case and gender when the article does not do so clearly enough.

## Declination with the definite article

- When an adjective follows the definite article, it almost always takes the ending -e or -en. The pattern is: nominative singular takes -e for all genders (der große Mann, die große Frau, das große Haus); accusative singular takes -e for masculine and neuter (den großen Mann, das große Haus) but -e for feminine (die große Frau); dative singular takes -en for all genders (dem großen Mann, der großen Frau, dem großen Haus); genitive singular takes -en for all genders (des großen Mannes, der großen Frau, des großen Hauses); all plural forms take -en regardless of case (die großen Männer, große Frauen, Häuser).

- A useful memory aid: with the definite article, you rarely encounter any ending except -e or -en, and most cases use -en.

## Declination with the indefinite article

- When an adjective follows an indefinite article or possessive pronoun (ein, eine, einen, mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer, Ihr), it must carry more of the work of showing case and gender. This is because ein is ambiguous in many forms—ein could be nominative or accusative neuter, for example.

- Nominative singular: ein großer Mann, eine große Frau, ein großes Haus. Notice the -er for masculine, -e for feminine, -es for neuter.

- Accusative singular: einen großen Mann, eine große Frau, ein großes Haus. The feminine and neuter do not change; only masculine adds -en.

- Dative and genitive singular: einem großen Mann, einer großen Frau, einem großen Haus; eines großen Mannes, einer großen Frau, eines großen Hauses. Here -en appears consistently.

- Plural forms in all cases: meine großen Bücher, unsere neuen Freunde, Ihre älteren Kollegen. The ending is always -en in the plural regardless of case or gender.

## Declination without an article

- Bare adjectives—those with no article at all—take the strong endings, which largely mirror the definite article forms but use different endings in some slots. Nominative singular: großer Mann, große Frau, großes Haus. Accusative: großen Mann, große Frau, großes Haus. Dative: großem Mann, großer Frau, großem Haus. Genitive: großen Mannes, großer Frau, großen Hauses.

- The key difference from the definite article pattern is that nominative and accusative use slightly different forms in masculine (großer vs. großen). This happens because the bare adjective must signal case and gender on its own.

- Plural unpreceded adjectives always take -e in nominative and accusative, and -en in dative and genitive: große Männer, große Häuser, großen Männern, großer Häuser.

## Spelling and morphology rules

- Most adjectives simply add -e, -en, -er, -es, -em, or -em to the base form. Adjectives ending in -el, -en, or -er often drop the -e from the stem when declining: ein dunkles Zimmer (not "ein dunkeles Zimmer"), ein dunkelbraunes Fell.

- Adjectives ending in a vowel (such as hoh-, blau-, treu-) also exist, and most follow the regular pattern: eine hohe Frau, ein hohes Haus, but hohes does not become "hoees"—you keep it simple.

- Adjectives that form the comparative or superlative also decline: ein größerer Mann, die größte Frau, das nächste Mal. The comparative -er and superlative -st endings come before the case ending: einen größeren Mann, mit den größten Freunden.

## Common learner mistakes

- Forgetting that the adjective must decline at all. English speakers often want to say "the big house" and not change the adjective, but German requires das große Haus.

- Confusing which ending goes with which article type. Students often apply indefinite article patterns to definite articles or vice versa. Remember: definite articles "support" the adjective by carrying case information, so the adjective can take simpler -e or -en forms. Indefinite articles and no article require the adjective to carry more information.

- Overgeneralizing -en as the universal ending. While -en is very common, nominative singular forms and some accusative feminine and neuter forms require -e, -er, -es, or -em instead.

- Mishandling plural forms. Learners sometimes apply singular patterns to plural. In the plural, the ending is almost always -en when an article is present, regardless of case (die großen Häuser, den großen Häusern, der großen Häuser).

- Mixing up predicate and attributive adjectives. A predicate adjective (Das Haus ist groß) never declines; an attributive one (Das große Haus) always does. Many learners add endings to predicate adjectives or forget them on attributive ones.

## Practice patterns and memory aids

- Work through sentences where the adjective appears in different cases and genders: Ich sehe einen schönen Mann (accusative masculine), eine schöne Frau (accusative feminine), ein schönes Kind (accusative neuter), dem schönen Mädchen (dative), der schönen Stadt (genitive feminine).

- Pay attention to articles as signals. The article often "announces" case and gender to the listener or reader, so the adjective ending reinforces this information rather than being entirely independent.

- Group learning by context: definite article sentences, indefinite article sentences, and bare adjective sentences. Master one group before moving to the next.
