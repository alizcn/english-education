The Perfekt tense is the most common way German speakers talk about completed actions in the past. It expresses what has happened or what you have done, and it is the conversational past tense used in everyday speech and informal writing.

## When to use Perfekt

Use Perfekt to describe past events or actions that feel connected to the present moment. It answers the question "What has happened?" or "What did you do?"

- Ich bin heute Morgen aufgewacht und habe Kaffee getrunken. (I woke up this morning and drank coffee.)
- Sie haben das Buch gelesen. (They have read the book.)
- Hast du deine Hausaufgaben gemacht? (Have you done your homework?)

In spoken German, Perfekt is strongly preferred over Präteritum (simple past) except with modal verbs and the verbs sein, haben, and werden. The Perfekt tense is flexible and works for any recent or distant past event in informal contexts.

## Basic formation: positive sentences

Perfekt uses an auxiliary verb (haben or sein) plus a past participle. The auxiliary verb takes the main conjugation position, and the past participle goes at the end of the clause.

- Most verbs use haben: Ich habe einen Film gesehen. (I have watched a film.)
- Movement and state-change verbs use sein: Ich bin nach Hause gegangen. (I have gone home.)
- Ich bin aufgewacht. (I have woken up.)

The past participle is formed by adding a prefix (usually ge-) and a suffix (-t or -en) to the verb stem. Weak verbs add -t, strong verbs add -en with possible vowel changes.

- Weak: machen → gemacht (made)
- Strong: sehen → gesehen (seen)
- Strong with vowel change: singen → gesungen (sung)

## Negative sentences

To negate a Perfekt sentence, place nicht before the past participle.

- Ich habe das nicht verstanden. (I did not understand that.)
- Sie sind nicht gekommen. (They did not come.)
- Wir haben das Spiel nicht gewonnen. (We did not win the game.)

Occasionally, if the negation applies to the whole clause, nicht appears earlier, but the participle still ends the sentence.

## Questions in Perfekt

Form questions by placing the auxiliary verb (haben or sein) at the beginning of the sentence, with the past participle still at the end.

- Hast du schon gegessen? (Have you already eaten?)
- Ist er angekommen? (Has he arrived?)
- Habt ihr die Party genossen? (Have you enjoyed the party?)

Yes-no questions follow this inversion pattern. Information questions begin with the question word, then the auxiliary, then the subject.

- Wann bist du aufgewacht? (When did you wake up?)
- Was hast du gekauft? (What did you buy?)

## Spelling and formation rules for past participles

Verbs with inseparable prefixes (be-, ent-, er-, ver-, zer-) do not add the ge- prefix to the participle.

- besuchen → besucht (visited), not *gebesucht
- erkennen → erkannt (recognized), not *geerkannt
- verderben → verdorben (spoiled), not *geverderben

Separable verbs insert ge- between the prefix and the verb stem.

- anfangen → angefangen (started)
- aufstehen → aufgestanden (got up)
- einkaufen → eingekauft (shopped)

Strong verbs show irregular past participles that must be memorized. Common examples include: sein → gewesen, haben → gehabt, gehen → gegangen, sehen → gesehen, nehmen → genommen, schreiben → geschrieben, trinken → getrunken, fahren → gefahren.

Many verbs ending in -ieren do not add ge-.

- studieren → studiert (studied)
- trainieren → trainiert (trained)

## Time expressions and context markers

Common time phrases used with Perfekt include gestern (yesterday), letzte Woche (last week), in den letzten Tagen (in the last few days), and vor zwei Monaten (two months ago).

- Ich habe gestern einen Brief geschrieben. (I wrote a letter yesterday.)
- Das ist vor drei Jahren passiert. (That happened three years ago.)
- Sie haben letzte Woche angerufen. (They called last week.)

The word schon (already) and gerade (just now) often appear in Perfekt sentences, especially in questions and statements about recent events.

- Hast du schon Mittag gegessen? (Have you already eaten lunch?)
- Ich bin gerade angekommen. (I have just arrived.)

## Common mistakes learners make

Beginners often use the wrong auxiliary verb. Remember: most verbs take haben, but verbs of movement (gehen, laufen, fahren) and state change (aufwachen, einschlafen) take sein.

- Ich bin ins Kino gegangen. (correct: I went to the cinema.)
- *Ich habe ins Kino gegangen. (incorrect)

Another error is forgetting that the past participle must go to the end of the clause, even when haben or sein appears earlier. The word order rule is firm.

- Ich habe das Buch gelesen. (correct: I have read the book.)
- *Ich habe gelesen das Buch. (incorrect)

Learners sometimes confuse weak and strong participles. Weak verbs always use -t, but strong verbs use -en and may show vowel changes. There is no way to predict strong forms, so they must be learned.

- singen → gesungen (not *gesingt)
- bringen → gebracht (not *gebringen)

Do not forget the ge- prefix on most participles, even though inseparable prefix verbs and -ieren verbs break this rule.

- spielen → gespielt (correct)
- *spielen → spielt (incorrect)

Finally, learners sometimes place the auxiliary verb in the wrong spot, especially in questions or subordinate clauses. In subordinate clauses, the auxiliary comes before the participle, not after.

- Ich weiß, dass er das Spiel gewonnen hat. (correct: I know that he won the game.)
- *Ich weiß, dass er hat das Spiel gewonnen. (incorrect)
