French adjectives must match the noun they describe in both gender and number. This agreement affects spelling, and the position of the adjective relative to the noun follows specific patterns that are essential for correct French writing and speaking.

## Gender and Number Agreement

- All adjectives change form to match the gender (masculine or feminine) and number (singular or plural) of the noun they modify.
- Masculine singular: un livre intéressant (an interesting book)
- Feminine singular: une histoire intéressante (an interesting story)
- Masculine plural: des livres intéressants (interesting books)
- Feminine plural: des histoires intéressantes (interesting stories)
- Some adjectives have identical masculine and feminine forms: facile (easy), moderne (modern), rapide (fast). These change only for number: un problème facile, une question facile, des problèmes faciles, des questions faciles.

## Common Spelling Patterns for Feminine Forms

- Most adjectives add -e to form the feminine: grand → grande, blond → blonde, intelligent → intelligente.
- Adjectives ending in -e remain unchanged: un homme célèbre, une femme célèbre.
- Adjectives ending in -al often change to -ale: normal → normale, special → spéciale.
- Adjectives ending in -er change to -ère: léger → légère, entier → entière.
- Some adjectives double the final consonant before adding -e: bon → bonne, gros → grosse, gentil → gentille.
- Adjectives ending in -x change to -se: heureux → heureuse, sérieux → sérieuse.

## Adjective Placement: Before or After the Noun

- Most descriptive adjectives in French follow the noun: une voiture rouge (a red car), un professeur compétent (a competent teacher), des chaussures confortables (comfortable shoes).
- Short, common adjectives often precede the noun: un beau jour (a beautiful day), une petite maison (a small house), un bon ami (a good friend), une jeune fille (a young girl).
- Common adjectives that typically precede nouns include: beau, bon, grand, petit, jeune, vieux, nouveau, joli, mauvais, long, court.
- Some adjectives change meaning depending on position: un ancien professeur (a former teacher) versus un professeur ancien (an ancient teacher), un cher ami (a dear friend) versus un ami cher (an expensive friend).

## Plural Forms and Regular Patterns

- Most adjectives form the plural by adding -s: un chat noir, des chats noirs; une robe noire, des robes noires.
- Adjectives ending in -s, -x, or -z do not change in the plural: un jour gris, des jours gris; un exercice heureux, des exercices heureux; un nez rose, des nez roses.
- Adjectives ending in -al typically change to -aux in the plural: un jour spécial, des jours spéciaux; une règle générale, des règles générales.
- Some adjectives ending in -au add -x: un gâteau beau, des gâteaux beaux.

## Common Learner Mistakes

- Forgetting to add the -e for feminine forms: "une maison grand" instead of "une maison grande."
- Adding agreement when the adjective is used as an adverb: "Elle parle rapide" (she speaks fast) should be "Elle parle rapidement" because rapidement is an adverb, not modifying a noun.
- Placing all adjectives before the noun: "une intéressante histoire" instead of "une histoire intéressante." Only short, common adjectives typically precede the noun.
- Failing to make plural adjectives agree: "des jours importante" instead of "des jours importants."
- Not recognizing that some adjectives are invariable: "des gens chic" (chic people) because chic does not change, not "des gens chics."

## Demonstrative and Possessive Adjectives

- Demonstrative adjectives (ce, cet, cette, ces) agree with the noun: ce livre (this book), cette maison (this house), ces enfants (these children). Note that ce becomes cet before a masculine singular noun starting with a vowel or mute h: cet ami (this friend), cet homme (this man).
- Possessive adjectives also agree: mon frère (my brother), ma sœur (my sister), mes enfants (my children). The form depends on the gender and number of the noun, not the possessor: son stylo (his or her pen), sa robe (his or her dress).
