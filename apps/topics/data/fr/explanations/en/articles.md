French has three types of articles that work differently from English. These small words are essential because they reveal the gender and number of nouns, and they tell you whether you're talking about something specific, something general, or an unspecified quantity. Understanding when and how to use each type prevents you from sounding unnatural in French.

## Definite Articles: le, la, les, l'

Definite articles refer to specific, known things. Use them when you mean "the" in English, but also in many cases where English uses no article at all.

- Masculine singular: le (le chat = the cat)
- Feminine singular: la (la maison = the house)
- Before a vowel or silent h: l' (l'ami = the friend, l'homme = the man)
- Plural for all genders: les (les chats, les maisons, les amis = the cats, the houses, the friends)

You use the definite article when talking about something specific: J'aime le café (I like coffee in general, or the specific coffee we're discussing). Le prof arrive demain (The teacher arrives tomorrow). Notice that French uses the definite article for general statements and habits, where English often uses no article.

## Indefinite Articles: un, une, des

Indefinite articles introduce something new or non-specific. They mean "a," "an," or "some."

- Masculine singular: un (un livre = a book)
- Feminine singular: une (une pomme = an apple)
- Plural: des (des livres, des pommes = some books, some apples)

Use these when you mention something for the first time or when you're not talking about a specific thing: Il y a une cafeteria au centre-ville (There is a café downtown). J'ai acheté des fleurs hier (I bought some flowers yesterday). The key is that the listener doesn't know which specific thing you mean yet.

## Partitive Articles: du, de la, des

Partitive articles express an unspecified quantity of something uncountable. They mean "some of" and only exist in French, not in English.

- Masculine: du (du pain = some bread, du lait = some milk)
- Feminine: de la (de la farine = some flour, de la confiture = some jam)
- Before a vowel: de l' (de l'eau = some water, de l'huile = some oil)
- Plural: des (des fruits = some fruits, des légumes = some vegetables)

Use partitive articles with uncountable nouns or when you cannot easily count the item: Je veux du café (I want some coffee). Elle a acheté de la viande (She bought some meat). Notice that English often omits "some," but French cannot leave out the partitive article.

## Definite versus Indefinite versus Partitive

Chooosing the right article depends on meaning. Compare: Je mange la pomme (I eat the apple — the specific one), Je mange une pomme (I eat an apple — any apple), Je mange de la pomme (I eat some apple — a portion of it). The third sentence sounds strange in French because pomme is usually countable, so you would normally say une pomme.

With mass nouns, the distinction matters: du chocolat (some chocolate, mass), un chocolat (a chocolate candy, countable). The definite article le chocolat can mean either "chocolate in general" or "the specific chocolate."

## Negation and Questions

In negative sentences, the indefinite and partitive articles often change. After ne... pas, indefinite and partitive articles become de (or d' before a vowel), while the definite article stays the same:

- Je mange un gâteau → Je ne mange pas de gâteau (I don't eat a cake → I don't eat any cake)
- Je veux du lait → Je ne veux pas de lait (I want milk → I don't want any milk)
- Je vois la porte → Je ne vois pas la porte (I see the door → I don't see the door)

In questions, articles stay the same as in positive sentences: Veux-tu du café? (Do you want some coffee?) Est-ce qu'il y a une pharmacie près d'ici? (Is there a pharmacy near here?)

## Common Mistakes

- Forgetting the article entirely: English allows "I like coffee," but French must say "J'aime le café," not "J'aime café."
- Using the wrong gender article: "la café" is wrong; café is masculine, so "le café" is correct.
- Mixing up partitive and indefinite: "Je veux du café" (I want some coffee) is correct, not "Je veux un café" (which means I want a cup of coffee, implying a countable serving).
- Thinking the partitive exists in English: There is no English equivalent to du, de la, or partitive des. You must include them in French even when English leaves them out.
- Using de instead of des in plural: "des amis" is correct; "de amis" is wrong. The s in des is part of the article, not separate.

## Articles Before Adjectives

When an adjective comes before the noun, the article still matches the noun's gender and number: les grands chevaux (the big horses), des petites fleurs (some small flowers). Some adjectives must come before the noun (like bon, petit, grand), and the article does not change position.
