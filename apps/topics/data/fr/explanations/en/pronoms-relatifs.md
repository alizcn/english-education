Relative pronouns link two clauses by referring back to a noun (the antecedent) in the main clause. In French, the choice between qui, que, dont, and où depends on the grammatical role the pronoun plays in the subordinate clause. Mastering these four pronouns unlocks the ability to write more complex and natural French sentences.

## Understanding the Four Relative Pronouns

Each relative pronoun has a specific grammatical function. Qui is the subject of the relative clause. Que is the direct object. Dont replaces the object of the preposition de. Où refers to a place or sometimes a time. The antecedent is the noun the pronoun refers back to, and this is always expressed in the main clause.

## QUI: The Subject of the Relative Clause

Use qui when the relative pronoun is the subject performing the action in the subordinate clause. Qui never changes its form; it is the same for masculine, feminine, singular, and plural antecedents.

- La femme qui parle est ma mère. (The woman who is speaking is my mother. Who = subject of parle.)
- Les livres qui sont sur la table appartiennent à Paul. (The books that are on the table belong to Paul. That = subject of sont.)
- C'est le professeur qui m'a aidé. (It is the teacher who helped me. Who = subject of a aidé.)

In questions, qui as subject remains the same: Qui qui arrive? becomes Qui arrive? (Who arrives?) The relative pronoun qui can also be used in questions without a clear antecedent, but in a relative clause, it always has one.

## QUE: The Direct Object of the Relative Clause

Use que when the relative pronoun is the direct object (the thing being acted upon) in the subordinate clause. Que becomes qu' before a vowel or h. The past participle must agree with the direct object when using the passé composé with que in a relative clause.

- Le film que j'ai vu hier était excellent. (The film that I saw yesterday was excellent. That = direct object of ai vu.)
- Les amies que tu as rencontrées sont sympathiques. (The friends that you met are nice. That = direct object of as rencontrées; rencontrées agrees with amies.)
- C'est la voiture qu'il achète. (It is the car that he is buying. That = direct object of achète.)

A common mistake is using qui instead of que for direct objects. Learners sometimes write "la personne qui j'aime" when the correct form is "la personne que j'aime" (the person whom I love). Remember: if the verb in the relative clause already has its own subject expressed or implied, use que for the direct object.

## DONT: The Object of the Preposition DE

Use dont to replace any phrase beginning with de: de + direct object, or a possessive relationship, or the object of a verb that requires de. Dont is the most challenging pronoun because English speakers do not have a direct equivalent.

- Le livre dont tu as besoin est à la bibliothèque. (The book that you need [of which you have need] is at the library. Dont = object of avoir besoin de.)
- L'homme dont la femme est docteur habite près d'ici. (The man whose wife is a doctor lives near here. Dont = possessive, showing the wife belongs to the man.)
- C'est la raison dont je suis fier. (It is the reason of which I am proud. Dont = object of de.)

Dont does not change form. A frequent error is attempting to use de + qui or de + que, as in "le livre de que j'ai besoin," which is incorrect. Another mistake is forgetting that dont already contains de, so you write "dont de" (redundant).

## OÙ: Place and Sometimes Time

Use où to refer to a place or, occasionally, a moment in time. Où is invariable. When où refers to time, the antecedent is typically a noun like jour, année, époque, or moment.

- C'est la ville où je suis né. (It is the city where I was born. Where = refers to a place.)
- La maison où ils habitent est très grande. (The house where they live is very large. Where = place.)
- Je me souviens du jour où tu as appelé. (I remember the day when you called. When = refers to time.)

Learners sometimes confuse où with que in time expressions. Use où only when the antecedent is a place or time noun. If you mean "in which" or "at which" after a non-place noun, you may need à laquelle, duquel, or another form instead, though où works for day, year, and period.

## Common Mistakes Learners Make

Confusing qui and que is the most frequent error. Qui is always the subject; que is always the direct object. Saying "la personne qui je connais" is wrong; it must be "la personne que je connais." Using de + qui or de + que instead of dont is another classic mistake. Many learners write "le tableau de qui je parle" when they should write "le tableau dont je parle." Forgetting that que becomes qu' before a vowel leads to phrases like "que est" instead of "qu'est." Finally, overusing où when a different preposition + relative pronoun form is needed; for instance, "le pays auquel" or "la situation à laquelle" may be required instead of où in certain contexts.

## Choosing the Right Pronoun: A Quick Decision Guide

Ask yourself what role the noun plays in the relative clause. If the noun is the subject (the thing doing the action), use qui. If the noun is the direct object (the thing being acted upon with no preposition before it), use que. If the noun is preceded by de in the relative clause, use dont instead. If the noun is a place, or occasionally a time expression (jour, année, époque), use où. When in doubt, identify the verb in the relative clause and determine whether your noun is its subject, its direct object, or follows a preposition like de. This will guide you to the correct pronoun every time.
